Amazon decides what you pay. We take that back.

Two settings are on by default in nearly every Amazon ad account. Both hand pricing control to Amazon. Most sellers have never turned them off, because most agencies never suggest it.

Setting one: dynamic bidding

Your bid is supposed to be a ceiling. With dynamic bidding on, it isn't. Amazon raises your bid in real time when it decides a click is likely to convert — sometimes well above what you set.

The logic sounds reasonable: pay more for better clicks. But you never see the individual adjustments, only the averaged cost at the end of the month. So the cost creeps and there's nothing obvious to point at.

The seller's experience of this is simple. Ad spend goes up. Sales stay flat. Nobody can explain it.

Setting two: placement multipliers

On top of the bid adjustment, you can add a percentage increase for specific placements — top of search, product pages. Agencies set these during a launch push and then forget about them.

They compound with dynamic bidding. A multiplier applied to an already inflated bid is where a lot of accounts quietly lose the most money.

What we do instead

  1. Turn both off

    Dynamic bidding down to fixed. Placement multipliers to zero. Your bid becomes an actual ceiling again.

  2. Set bids top down, by hand

    Start high enough to hold position, then walk each bid down deliberately while watching what it does to volume. No algorithm, no automated rules.

  3. Rebuild the account structure

    Most accounts are still structured for the revenue they had years ago. We separate what should be separate so bids can actually be managed independently.

  4. Manage it weekly, forever

    Manual bidding only works if someone is actually doing it. This is the ongoing work, and it's why we cap how many accounts we take.

Why this is slower on purpose

Automated bid management scales. One person can run fifty accounts if software makes the decisions. That's the model most agencies are built on, and it's why they can charge a percentage of your spend and still make money.

Manual management doesn't scale that way. It takes real hours per account per week. The tradeoff is that a person is looking at your numbers and making judgment calls that an algorithm optimizing for Amazon's definition of success won't make.

This is the whole argument. If you'd rather have software run it, there are cheaper options and we'd genuinely point you at them.
